Where the FFT path spends its time

An 8192-tap impulse response on the partitioned-ir branch, timed one audio callback at a time. The FFT path doesn't do a little work on every callback. It does almost nothing on seven callbacks, then all of its transform work on the eighth.

Each bar is one callback's CPU time as a share of its 1.33 ms deadline. Shipping costs about the same every time. The FFT path gathers input for 512 samples, which is eight callbacks, then transforms it, multiplies it against all 15 partitions of the tail, and transforms it back, all inside the callback that completes the 512 samples.

45,000 callbacks per subject. Shipping forms one cluster near 4.2%. The FFT path forms two: about 7/8 of its callbacks near 0.2%, and 1/8 near 2.2%. That second cluster is why block_p99_percent exists. The 99th percentile falls inside the transform cluster, so the FFT path's p99 (2.24%) is simply what a transform callback costs, not a rare outlier.

Is it predictable? Completely.

Nothing in the FFT path branches on sample values. It does the same transforms and multiply-adds whether the input is silence or white noise, and whatever the impulse response contains. Denormals are flushed to zero, so near-silence has no slow path either. The spike always lands on the callback that completes 512 samples, which here means callbacks 8, 16, 24 and so on after a reset.

How the spike grows with IR length

The quiet callbacks cost the same at every length, because they only run the direct head (256 taps up to 2048, then 512). The transform callback grows with the number of partitions. At 1024 taps and below it costs more than a shipping callback, even though the FFT path's average is lower. That's why the p99 was worse than shipping's at 512 taps on every machine, and at 1024 on the ARM boards.

Larger callbacks flatten it

The spike comes from the ratio between the FFT block (512 samples at 8192 taps) and the host's callback size. With 32-frame callbacks the transform lands once in 16, and costs 4.4% of a deadline that is half as long. At 512 frames or more every callback runs a transform, so every callback costs the same and the p99 sits right on the average. Shipping doesn't care about callback size.

What a transform callback actually does

At 8192 taps the branch uses 512-sample blocks and a 1024-point FFT. The first 512 taps run directly, which keeps latency at zero. The remaining 7680 are split into 15 partitions. When these traces were taken, each transform callback ran one forward FFT, 15 × 1024 complex multiply-adds, one inverse FFT, and added 1023 samples into the output ring. Two things could lower or flatten the spike:

Only half the spectrum is needed. The input is real, so bins 513–1023 mirror bins 1–511, and the multiply-add loop was doing roughly twice the work it needed. Since done: partitioned-ir commit a09e360 multiplies and stores only bins 0–512. Output is bit-identical, and at 8192 taps the transform callback dropped from 29.3 to 20.4 µs on the M2. The p99 fell 28–29% on the M2 and the Pi 500, and 23% on the Tinker Board.

The work could be spread out. Fourteen of the 15 partition multiplies use input blocks that arrived earlier. They could run during the seven quiet callbacks, leaving only the two FFTs and one multiply for the eighth. Some convolution engines do this, at the cost of extra code. Not done.
